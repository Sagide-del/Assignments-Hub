Grade 7 Mathematics Revision: How to Help Your Child with Fractions

Assignment Hub Team · Updated 2026-11-13
06
MathematicsAH

The short answer

Choose one difficult skill, work through one example, then let the learner answer a similar question without looking. Ask them to explain the method and revisit it later in the week.

Start with one skill, not the whole textbook

When a Grade 7 learner says “I cannot do Mathematics,” ask for one example rather than assigning another full paper. The difficulty may be a specific step: recognising equivalent fractions, finding a common denominator, or reading what a question asks. Sit together for ten minutes. Ask the learner to solve one familiar question without looking at the worked answer. Notice where they hesitate. That is your starting point. This approach supports CBC classroom learning without asking a parent to become a full-time tutor.

A parent script that keeps the learner thinking

Try: “Show me what you understand so far.” Follow with “What does the denominator tell us?” and “Could we draw this?” Give the learner time to answer. If you explain immediately, you learn less about the missing skill. Avoid comparisons with classmates and labels such as careless or lazy. Praise a specific action: checking a denominator, drawing equal parts, or correcting an earlier mistake. The aim is to make the next attempt possible, not to finish every question in the exercise book.

Worked example: three quarters plus one eighth

Consider 3/4 + 1/8. Quarters and eighths are different-sized parts, so first express both amounts in eighths. Since 3/4 = 6/8, the addition becomes 6/8 + 1/8 = 7/8. We add the numerators because the parts now have the same size; the denominator stays 8. If the learner adds top and bottom to get 4/12, ask whether that answer makes sense. Adding a positive amount to 3/4 should produce a larger number, while 4/12 equals 1/3. Estimating helps reveal the mistake before checking the marking scheme.

Move from a worked example to independent practice

After explaining the first example, close it. Ask the learner to try 1/2 + 1/4 and explain each step. The answer is 3/4 because 1/2 is equivalent to 2/4. Then try 2/3 + 1/6: rewrite 2/3 as 4/6, giving 5/6. If a step fails, return to a drawing or equivalent-fraction example. Two carefully corrected questions can reveal more than twenty copied solutions. End by asking the learner to explain why we do not add the denominators.

A manageable weekly revision routine

On Monday, use ten minutes to identify one fraction difficulty. On Tuesday, work through one example and answer two similar questions independently. On Thursday, revisit the idea without opening the notes first. On Saturday, mix a few fraction questions with another recently taught topic. Keep breaks and ordinary homework in the plan. This combines active recall, revisiting after a gap and mixed practice; it is a routine to adapt, not a guarantee of a particular grade.

What to ask the teacher when a difficulty persists

Keep two examples of corrected work and ask the teacher which prerequisite needs attention. A learner may need multiplication facts or understanding of equal parts before more advanced fraction operations. If reading the question is the main difficulty, practise explaining its meaning before calculating. Discuss persistent difficulties with the school rather than relying on more online worksheets alone. The teacher can connect home practice with what is currently being taught.

Questions parents ask

How can I help my Grade 7 child with Mathematics at home?

Choose one difficult skill, work through one example, then let the learner answer a similar question without looking. Ask them to explain the method and revisit it later in the week.

Why should we not add the denominators when adding fractions?

The denominator describes the size of the parts. Convert to equal-sized parts first, then add their counts. For example, 3/4 + 1/8 becomes 6/8 + 1/8 = 7/8.
