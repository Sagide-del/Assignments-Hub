CBC Grading Levels Explained: What Do EE, ME, AE, and BE Mean?

Assignment Hub Team · 2026-11-10
CBC explained

What the letters are trying to tell you

CBC reporting is designed to describe what a learner can demonstrate, not simply to rank a child. Many school reports use four performance descriptions: EE for Exceeding Expectation, ME for Meeting Expectation, AE for Approaching Expectation and BE for Below Expectation. The exact layout, assessment task and wording can differ between schools, so a parent should read the teacher comment and the task beside the descriptor. The important question is not “Why is my child not first?” It is “What can my child do now, and what is the next skill to practise?”

EE: Exceeding Expectation

EE usually means the learner has demonstrated the intended skill confidently and can often apply it in a new situation. In Mathematics, that may mean solving a familiar problem and explaining the method. In English, it may mean selecting evidence and communicating a clear idea. EE is not a reason to stop practising. It is an opportunity to deepen learning through explanation, challenge questions, reading and real-life application. Ask the learner to teach one idea back to you. Teaching exposes whether understanding is secure and builds confidence without turning home into an exam room.

ME: Meeting Expectation

ME means the learner is working at the expected level for the assessed skill. This is healthy progress. Rather than pushing immediately for extra worksheets, help the learner protect the habits that produced it: regular attendance, short revision sessions, sleep, questions in class and correction of mistakes. A useful weekly routine is one brief retrieval quiz, one worked example and a five-minute conversation about what felt difficult. Progress becomes more reliable when a child practises before forgetting, rather than only when a test is announced.

AE and BE: a signal for a plan, not a label

AE means the learner is close to the expected skill but needs targeted support; BE means the evidence on that particular assessment is below the expected level. Neither tells you a child’s intelligence, future pathway or value. Start by finding the smallest missing step. A Mathematics AE may come from fractions, not from “being bad at maths”. A Science BE may come from vocabulary, reading the question carefully or a missed practical lesson. Ask the teacher for one or two precise examples. Then make the practice small enough to complete: ten minutes, three questions, immediate correction and a second attempt two days later.

A calm parent response

Begin with curiosity: “Which question felt confusing?” “Show me where you got stuck.” Avoid comparing siblings or classmates. Review the report after the child has eaten and rested, not in the heat of collection day. Agree on one priority subject and one skill for the next two weeks. Keep a simple record of the date, skill, score and correction. If the same issue continues, share that record with the teacher. It gives the teacher useful evidence and makes support more specific. If a report shows a sudden drop across several subjects, also check wellbeing, attendance, vision, workload and changes at home.

Using digital practice responsibly

Assignment Hub can help a learner complete published assignments, receive instant feedback on objective questions and revisit explanations. Use the feedback as a conversation starter rather than a verdict. A good routine is: attempt without notes, check the explanation, write one correction in the learner’s own words, and return to a similar question later. Parents should still rely on the school and teacher for formal assessment decisions. Digital practice supports learning; it does not replace a teacher’s professional judgement.
